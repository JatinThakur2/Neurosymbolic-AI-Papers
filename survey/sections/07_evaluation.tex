\textbf{What abstracts name.} Table~\ref{tab:entities} summarises the entities named in abstracts. The two coders agreed well on model families and benchmarks (mean Jaccard \cnt{jaclm} and \cnt{jacbenchmark}) and less well on engines (\cnt{jacengine}), so engine counts are approximate. Only \cnt{lmstudies} studies (\cnt{lmstudiespct}\%) name a model family in the abstract; the counts describe reporting in abstracts, not usage. Among them, GPT-4-family models are the most named (\cnt{lmGPT4}). Proprietary and open-weight families are used side by side: \cnt{lmprop} studies name a proprietary family and \cnt{lmopen} an open-weight one, while \cnt{lmproponly} name only proprietary families. The Python interpreter (\cnt{engPython}) and Lean (\cnt{engLean}) are the most named engines, which reflects the weight of program-aided reasoning and formal theorem proving.

\textbf{Benchmarks are fragmented.} \cnt{benchstudies} studies name at least one benchmark in the abstract, and together they name \cnt{benchdistinct} distinct benchmarks. Of these, \cnt{benchsingle} (\cnt{benchsinglepct}\%) are named by a single study, and only \cnt{benchfiveplus} are named by five or more. miniF2F is the only benchmark named by more than 20 studies (\cnt{benchminif2f}). This conclusion no longer depends on a fixed dictionary, because the vocabulary is open. Abstracts under-report benchmarks, though, so the true overlap is somewhat higher.

\textbf{A results-level view of the one shared benchmark.} Every included abstract that mentions miniF2F belongs to D1. Of the \cnt{mfmention} D1 studies, \cnt{mfreported} state a miniF2F number in the abstract and \cnt{mfcomparable} state an absolute pass rate (Fig.~\ref{fig:minif2f}). The best reported rate rises from \cnt{mfmax2022}\% in 2022~\cite{wu2022autoformalization} to \cnt{mfmax2025}\% in 2025~\cite{ospanov2025apollo} and \cnt{mfmax2026}\% in 2026~\cite{varambally2026hilbert}. These numbers are not a leaderboard. Sampling budgets range from a single attempt to thousands, splits and formal languages differ, and audits find mis-specified statements and evaluation loopholes in the benchmark itself~\cite{ospanov2025minif2f,ammanamanchi2026faults}. The figure shows two things: the most shared benchmark of the field is close to saturation, and results are reported in a way that prevents direct comparison.

\textbf{Formalisation faithfulness is often unmeasured, and abstracts under-report it.} For A1 and A2 studies whose model writes formal statements, correctness depends on whether the formal statement means what the problem says. Of \cnt{faithn} such studies, \cnt{faithyes} (\cnt{faithyespct}\%) report the accuracy or faithfulness of the formalisation separately from end-task accuracy in the abstract, \cnt{faithno} do not, and \cnt{faithunc} are unclear. Abstracts are a weak basis for this indicator, which is why we checked it in full texts. In the random sample of \cnt{ftn} A1/A2 studies (\cnt{ftfull} with full text), \cnt{ftfaithrel} have a model that writes formal statements; \cnt{ftfaithyes} of them (\cnt{ftfaithyespct}\%, 95\% CI \cnt{ftfaithyeslo}--\cnt{ftfaithyeshi}) report faithfulness somewhere in the paper, but only \cnt{ftfaithabs} say so in the abstract. Agreement between abstract and full-text codes was $\kappa=\cnt{ftfaithkappa}$. Faithfulness is therefore measured in about half of the formalising studies, not in a small minority, but it is rarely a headline result, and the abstract-level figures by domain---reporting is most common in D1 (\cnt{faithD1yes} of \cnt{faithD1}), where autoformalisation is itself a task~\cite{li2024autoformalize,liu2025rethinking,chen2026reform}, and almost absent in logical reasoning (\cnt{faithD2yes} of \cnt{faithD2}) and mathematical problem solving (\cnt{faithD7yes} of \cnt{faithD7})---describe what authors choose to foreground rather than what they measure.

\textbf{How reliable are abstract-based check strengths?} In the same sample, full text and abstract agreed on check strength for \cnt{ftAtwoagree} of \cnt{ftAtwon} A2 studies, so the split between exact and empirical checking is robust. Overall agreement was lower ($\kappa=\cnt{ftcheckkappa}$) because of A1: for \cnt{ftAoneexec} of \cnt{ftAonen} A1 studies the full-text coder counted the execution that computes the answer as an empirical or exact check, whereas the protocol codes it \texttt{none} (Section~\ref{sec:method-coding}). The disagreement is mostly about the definition rather than about what the papers do; we have made the definition explicit.

\textbf{Artifacts are usually released.} In the random sample of \cnt{artrandn} stage-1 studies, \cnt{artrandyes} have an author-released code, model or data artifact, \cnt{artrandno} have none that could be found and \cnt{artrandunc} could not be determined (Table~\ref{tab:artifacts}). The estimated release rate is \cnt{artrandrate}\% (95\% CI \cnt{artrandlo}--\cnt{artrandhi}). It is \cnt{artrandknown}\% among determinable studies, and \cnt{artrandupper}\% if all unclear studies released. In the A7 census, \cnt{artasevenyes} of \cnt{artasevenn} studies release an artifact. Some groups of sampled studies are small, so per-architecture differences should not be over-read. The pilot estimated release from links in PDF text and reported a markedly lower rate, which shows how much that method under-counts.
