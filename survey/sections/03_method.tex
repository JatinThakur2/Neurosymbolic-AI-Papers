\section{Review Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Design and reporting standard}
We conducted a scoping review in the sense of Arksey and O'Malley~\cite{arksey2005scoping}: the aim is to map the extent, range and nature of the evidence, not to estimate effects. Reporting follows PRISMA-ScR~\cite{tricco2018prisma}; Appendix~\ref{app:prisma} maps every checklist item to its location. Planning and conduct also follow the systematic-mapping guidance of software engineering~\cite{petersen2008systematic,kitchenham2007guidelines}. The protocol (version 2, released with the data) fixes the eligibility rules, the screening and adjudication procedure and the coding scheme. It was not registered in advance. It replaced a pilot version that searched only the curated corpus, and it was amended once more during revision; every amendment is listed in the protocol file and in Section~\ref{sec:method-changes}, and the decision files record which version each decision applied. Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} summarises the workflow.

\subsection{Eligibility criteria}
\label{sec:method-eligibility}
A study is included when all of I1--I4 hold (Table~\ref{tab:criteria}). I1 restricts the population to archival research papers at ten venues, 2020--2026: the main conference of ACL, EMNLP and NAACL; ICLR; ICML; NeurIPS (main and Datasets \& Benchmarks tracks); the technical and special research tracks of AAAI and IJCAI; TMLR; and IEEE TPAMI. The rule is ``every peer-reviewed research track of the venue'': NeurIPS Datasets \& Benchmarks is included because benchmarks with symbolic oracles are part of the map; special research tracks of AAAI and IJCAI are research tracks with full review; and survey-track papers, where they exist, are screened and removed by E5. ACL Findings volumes are excluded. They are archival, but they exist only at the NLP venues and are accepted under a different bar, so including them would enlarge the NLP side of the map by construction. The cost is that well-known Findings papers, such as Logic-LM~\cite{pan2023logiclm}, are out of scope; we cite them as background only.

I2 and I3 together require that a language model and an explicit symbolic component \emph{interact}. Six rules make this boundary operational.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{R1 (language model).} A neural sequence model trained or pretrained on natural-language, mathematical-text or source-code corpora, at any scale from LSTM language models to LLMs, or a vision-language model built on one. A sequence model trained only on task-generated symbolic data (for example, a transformer trained from scratch to emit expression trees) does not qualify.
  \item \emph{R2 (interaction).} The output of one component is the input, constraint, verdict or training signal of the other. Portfolios that merely choose between a model and a solver do not qualify. Evaluation studies qualify when a symbolic system generates the problems or certifies the answers the model is scored against. A frozen (vision-)language model used as a perception front end qualifies when it emits the symbols---labels, predicates, scene graphs---that a symbolic core reasons over (architecture A7); E2 applies only when the model is a feature extractor for a neural pipeline, background, or a baseline.
  \item \emph{R3 (symbolic component).} A system that represents knowledge or computation in an explicit formal language and manipulates it by rule-governed procedures: solvers, provers and proof assistants, logic and answer-set programs, planners, model checkers, interpreters executing model-written programs, computer algebra, symbolic search, and grammars, automata or static analyses that constrain or check the model. Natural-language reasoning chains, text retrieval stores and neural modules without a formal representation do not qualify.
  \item \emph{R4 (program and query generation).} For code generation evaluated by tests, text-to-SQL and knowledge-base question answering by query generation, executing the generated program does not by itself satisfy I3; an additional symbolic component (verifier, type or dataflow analysis, grammar-based checker, symbolic search) is required. When a program is the \emph{vehicle} for another task---mathematics, tables, puzzles, planning, robot control, visual reasoning---I3 is satisfied.
  \item \emph{R5 (knowledge graphs).} A knowledge graph counts only when the method performs logical inference over it; retrieving triples into a prompt does not.
  \item \emph{R6 (tool use).} Generic multi-tool frameworks do not satisfy I3 unless a specific formal engine under R3 is the object of the integration.
\end{itemize}
R4 draws the most contested boundary. We include program-aided reasoning because the interpreter executes a formal artefact the model wrote and computes the answer by rule-governed evaluation---the same division of labour as translating a problem into logic for a solver. We exclude plain code generation because there the program \emph{is} the output, and executing it only tests it. Many neurosymbolic researchers would draw the line elsewhere, so Section~\ref{sec:taxonomy-sens} reports every main finding with and without the \cnt{Fthreen} studies whose formalism is a general-purpose program.

\subsection{Information sources}
\label{sec:method-sources}
We used two sources (Table~\ref{tab:sources}).

\textbf{Source A} is a curated, venue-organised corpus of \cnt{corpusrecords} neurosymbolic records~\corpuscite{} (commit \texttt{9a42645}), built by matching venue title lists against neurosymbolic and adjacent-field keywords. It covers seven of the ten venues; its title also lists JMLR, but at this commit it holds no JMLR records. It is the only source for venue-years without machine-readable abstracts: AAAI 2020 and 2026, IJCAI 2025, TMLR and TPAMI. Because its records were selected by title, it cannot by itself support claims about recall.

\textbf{Source B} is the accepted-paper metadata, with abstracts, of the other eight venues. It combines the Paper Copilot paper lists (commit \texttt{9fcfd12}, with the last plain-text versions of ICLR 2025 and 2026) and the main-conference volumes of the ACL Anthology (commit \texttt{73f3b3a}). Only accepted papers in in-scope tracks are kept. Stage~1 of the search used \cnt{sourcebvtwo} records (\cnt{sourcebtitles} distinct titles; one title appears at two venue-years). During revision we added \cnt{srcbaddaaai} papers from the AAAI special research tracks, to apply I1 consistently, and \cnt{srcbaddanth} main-conference papers that the Paper Copilot lists lacked (mostly EMNLP 2023 and 2024 and NAACL 2025), found by comparing every ACL, EMNLP and NAACL volume with the Anthology. Source B therefore holds \cnt{sourceb} records. \ifanonymous One of the authors\else The author of this review\fi{} curates Source A (\ifanonymous declared to the editor\else see Competing Interests\fi); Source B is independent of \ifanonymous the authors\else the author\fi.

\subsection{Search}
\label{sec:method-search}
The search ran in two stages, both on the title and abstract of every Source B record (Appendix~\ref{app:query}). A record is retrieved when it matches both a language-model block and a symbolic block.

\textbf{Stage 1} used the query of protocol v2. The language-model block includes \emph{transformer}, \emph{seq2seq}, \emph{prompting} and \emph{code model}, so that pre-LLM work is not missed. The symbolic block includes solvers, provers, formal languages, planners, program synthesis, interpreters, DSLs and logical forms. It retrieved \cnt{queryhits} records. Merged with the \cnt{corpusscreened} unique Source A records (\cnt{overlap} appear in both), this gave \cnt{union} records to screen.

\textbf{Stage 2} answers the observation that the stage-1 symbolic block lacked obvious terms. It adds model checking, NuSMV, Dafny, Verus, Datalog, \emph{planner(s)}, planning domain/language/problem, MILP, integer and linear programming, optimisation models, Pyomo, Gurobi, \emph{proof(s)}, SQL, execution traces, TLA+ and Alloy to the symbolic block, and recent model names (Qwen, Mistral, Gemini, DeepSeek, Claude, GPT) and \emph{reasoning models} and \emph{language agents} to the language-model block. The augmented query retrieves \cnt{augmentedhits} records; the \cnt{searchtwo} that stage 1 had not screened (including the hits among the records added to Source B) were screened in stage 2 by the same procedure. Matching on abstracts rather than titles is what removes the dependence on the word ``neurosymbolic'' that a title-keyword corpus has (Section~\ref{sec:trends}).

\subsection{Selection of sources of evidence}
\label{sec:method-selection}
Every record was screened on title and abstract by two independent LLM screeners. Each saw only the protocol, its batch and a viewer, and was forbidden to open other project files or other screeners' outputs. Each screener returned \texttt{include}, \texttt{exclude} with a code, or \texttt{unsure}. When both said include, or both said exclude, that decision was final. Every other combination---any \texttt{unsure} or any conflict---went to an adjudicator. The adjudicator saw both decisions and rationales, could read the full text, and had to decide; it could not answer \texttt{unsure}. Each adjudication records its evidence: abstract, local full text or a URL. All prompts are released.

\textbf{Backward snowballing}~\cite{wohlin2014guidelines}. The reference lists of the \cnt{snowseeds} included studies with a local full text (the Source A records whose PDFs the corpus stores) were matched against all Source B records, not only search hits. A record cited by at least one of them and not already screened became a candidate. The \cnt{snowcand} candidates went through the same two-screener process. We did not snowball forward, and backward snowballing covers only a tenth of the included studies. Instead of extending snowballing, we measured what all search routes together miss, with the recall audit and the known-item check below.

\textbf{Recall audit.} Source B records that no search stage retrieved and that were not snowball candidates were stratified by which query block they match: language-model terms only, symbolic terms only, or neither. Every one of the \cnt{sourceb} records is accounted for: \cnt{srcbinunion} were in the stage-1 union, \cnt{srcbsnow} were snowball candidates, one was a duplicate title, one shared a record identifier with another, \cnt{srcbaddedtwo} were added in revision and entered stage 2 or the strata, and \cnt{srcbauditpop} formed the stage-1 audit population. Stage~1 drew seeded random samples of 200, 100 and 100 records from these strata; \cnt{auditretrieved} of them were later retrieved by the stage-2 query, and such records leave the strata because they count as search hits. During revision we drew a further \cnt{audextnein} records from the neither stratum and \cnt{audextlmn} from the language-model-only stratum, to narrow the interval. All \cnt{auditscreened}+\cnt{auditextn} audit records were screened by two independent screeners, and every record that either screener did not exclude was adjudicated. For the full search the strata hold $N=\cnt{audtwolmN}$, \cnt{audtwosymN} and \cnt{audtwoneiN} records.

Let $N_h$, $n_h$ and $x_h$ be the stratum size, sample size and eligible count, and $F$ the number of eligible Source B studies found by the search stages and snowballing (\cnt{foundtwo}). The number of missed studies is $M=\sum_h N_h\pi_h$, where $\pi_h$ is the eligible proportion of stratum $h$, and recall is $F/(F+M)$. We draw $10^5$ samples of $\pi_h$ from independent Jeffreys posteriors $\mathrm{Beta}(x_h+\tfrac12, n_h-x_h+\tfrac12)$ and report the posterior median of recall with its 2.5\% and 97.5\% quantiles. Point estimate and interval therefore come from the same posterior; the plug-in estimate $F/(F+\sum_h N_h x_h/n_h)$, which treats an empty stratum as containing no eligible study, is given for reference only. Studies found by the audit are included as ``identified by other methods''.

\textbf{Known-item check.} As an independent test of recall we used the reference list of a recent survey that played no part in designing our query~\cite{cheng2025empowering} (arXiv version 4). We kept the \cnt{kiref} references published at our venues in 2020--2026 and traced each one: whether it is in Source B, which route screened it, and the final decision. The \cnt{kiscreened} references in Source B that no route had screened were screened by the same two-screener procedure.


\subsection{Data charting}
\label{sec:method-coding}
Two independent LLM coders charted every included study from its title and abstract, under the same blinding rules. Every study on which they disagreed in any coded field was adjudicated; the adjudicator could consult the full text. Table~\ref{tab:codebook} gives the codebook.

The \emph{architecture} code is assigned by a decision tree applied in a fixed order (A6, A5, A1, A2, A4, A7, A3), stopping at the first match. The order was fixed after the pilot to resolve the boundary that was unstable there, so it is a design choice rather than a finding; Section~\ref{sec:taxonomy-sens} tests how much the results depend on it. Whenever a proof assistant, solver or executor checks the model's outputs and its verdict selects, repairs, filters or rewards candidates, the study is A2, even when a learned value model guides a proof search. A3 is reserved for symbolic structure that steers generation but gives no verdict on the output. Because ``checking'' covers both sound formal verdicts and execution- or data-based tests, every study also receives a \emph{check strength}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{exact}: a sound formal verdict on the final output, from a proof assistant, certificate checker or model checker;
  \item \texttt{empirical}: a check that can pass wrong outputs, such as tests, execution results compared with expectations, fit to data or simulator success;
  \item \texttt{none}: the symbolic component never checks the final output.
\end{itemize}
Check strength concerns a \emph{verdict} on an output. When an engine computes the answer from the model's formal representation, as in A1, that execution is not a check, and the code is \texttt{none} unless something also checks the result.

Coders also recorded the language models, symbolic engines and benchmarks named in the abstract (open vocabulary). For A1/A2 studies whose model writes formal statements, they recorded whether the abstract reports the faithfulness of the formalisation separately from end-task accuracy.

\textbf{Supplementary coding.} The decision tree mixes two questions: what a paper contributes and how its system is built. A6, in particular, is defined by contribution type. Two further independent coders therefore recorded, for every included study, (i)~its \emph{contribution type}---method, benchmark or analysis---and (ii)~\emph{every} integration mechanism its own system uses (A1--A5, A7), judged independently of the decision-tree order. Contribution-type disagreements were adjudicated; a mechanism counts when both coders listed it. These codes let us restrict architecture analyses to method papers and re-apply the decision tree in other orders.

\textbf{Full-text check.} Architecture and check strength come from abstracts. To measure what this costs, an LLM coder recoded check strength and faithfulness reporting from the full text of a seeded random sample of \cnt{ftn} A1/A2 studies; we compare these codes with the abstract-based ones (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}).

\subsection{Further indicators}
\label{sec:method-indicators}
\textbf{Entities.} Model names were mapped to 15 families or roles and engine names to 12 families with documented patterns. A study counts as naming a family when either coder listed a matching string; these union counts are upper bounds on what the abstracts state. Benchmark names were normalised by case, punctuation and split suffixes (``miniF2F-test'' and ``MiniF2F'' coincide). Agreement between the coders' entity sets is reported as mean Jaccard similarity.

\textbf{Artifact release.} Because links in PDF text under-count releases, availability was checked on the web. LLM web checkers searched for an author-released repository, model or dataset for each study in a simple random sample of 150 studies drawn from the stage-1 inclusions, and for every A7 study of stage 1 (a census, because A7 is small). Third-party re-implementations, empty repositories and promises of future release do not count. Studies whose check could not be completed are reported as unclear, and rates are given both with unclear studies counted as not released and among determinable studies only.

\textbf{Reported results.} For every D1 study, and every other study whose abstract mentions miniF2F, an LLM extractor copied the miniF2F result stated in the abstract with the supporting quote. We verified each quote against the abstract programmatically and classified each result as an absolute pass rate or not (for example, a gain over a baseline, an autoformalisation accuracy or a variant benchmark); the classification is released.

\subsection{Synthesis and statistics}
\label{sec:method-synthesis}
We report frequencies with Wilson 95\% intervals~\cite{wilson1927probable} and cross-tabulations, followed by a narrative synthesis per domain. Association between architecture and domain is measured by Cram\'er's $V$ with Bergsma's bias correction~\cite{bergsma2013bias}, tested by permutation ($10^4$ label shuffles), compared with the mean $V$ under the permutation null, and recomputed with each domain left out in turn.

\textbf{Trends.} Architecture shares are reported by period with Wilson intervals, for all venues and for the constant-venue ICLR+ICML panel. Because the venue mix changes over time---the NLP venues contribute no 2026 studies---we also fit, for each architecture, a logistic regression of ``study uses this architecture'' on publication year with venue fixed effects, and report the odds ratio per year with a Wald 95\% interval.

\textbf{Growth.} Growth is normalised in two ways at venue-years with constant coverage: by all accepted papers, and by accepted papers that match the language-model block of the query, which asks whether symbolic integration grows as a share of language-model research. The panels are ICLR, ICML, NeurIPS, ACL and EMNLP for 2020--2025, and ICLR and ICML for 2020--2026. Rate ratios between years come with exact conditional (Clopper--Pearson) 95\% intervals, because the 2020 counts are small. As a terminology control we count the included studies whose title, or title and abstract, contains ``neurosymbolic'' or ``neural-symbolic''. Because the language-model block contains vocabulary that became common only after 2022 (``LLM'', ``prompting'', ``in-context learning''), we also recompute growth counting only studies whose abstracts match the language-model vocabulary already common before 2022.

\textbf{Sensitivity analyses.} We repeat the main findings (i)~without studies whose formalism is a general-purpose program (F3), (ii)~on method papers only, (iii)~under three alternative decision-tree orders applied to the supplementary mechanism codes and (iv)~on the inclusions confirmed by the human check. No quality appraisal of individual studies was performed; this is optional in PRISMA-ScR and outside the aims of a map.

\subsection{LLM-based reviewers and their disclosure}
\label{sec:method-ai}
Screening, adjudication, coding, web checking, result extraction and the full-text check were carried out by instances of Anthropic's Claude models running as independent agents with tool access. Each received only the protocol, a written task prompt and its batch. Model versions were not recorded uniformly for every batch; this is a limitation we report. The protocol, the prompts and the analysis pipeline were drafted with an AI assistant, which also orchestrated the agents and made two decisions itself, both marked in the decision files: one audit screening decision and one coding dispute. Reversing the audit decision changes the recall estimate from \cnt{recalltwo} to \cnt{recallassist}; replacing the coding decision with the coders' code moves one study from A2 to A3. \ifanonymous The authors are\else The author is\fi{} responsible for the protocol and prompts and accountable for every decision.

Three consequences follow. First, the agreement statistics measure the consistency of independent runs of models of the same family under the same prompt, not human inter-rater reliability; they mainly show that the rules are applied deterministically. Second, model reviewers may share systematic errors that two humans would not, so high agreement does not rule out a shared bias. Third, the \cnt{singleadjincl} inclusions (\cnt{singleadjinclpct}\% of those from screening) that an adjudicator decided rest on a single model judgement that no agreement statistic covers. Section~\ref{sec:method-human} describes the human checks designed to measure these errors. Everything a reviewer saw and decided is released, so any decision can be audited and overturned; re-running the analysis propagates the change to every number in the paper. The decisions themselves cannot be regenerated exactly, because model versions were not recorded.

\subsection{Human verification}
\label{sec:method-human}
\textbf{Author's check.} A simple random 30\% sample of the records screened by two LLM reviewers in stage~1 (751 records) and a 30\% sample of the stage-1 inclusions (198 studies) were released as blinded sheets showing only title, venue, year, abstract and link. \ifanonymous One author\else The author\fi{} screened and coded them independently of the LLM decisions, applying the protocol. The sample was drawn before the revision-2 search, so it covers the stage-1 decisions.

\textbf{Independent second rater.} Because \ifanonymous the first rater is an author who\else the author\fi{} designed the protocol and curates Source A, a second person with no stake in the review rated a subset of the author's samples blind: 150 screening records, stratified to contain 75 inclusions and 75 exclusions of the pipeline (inclusions are rare, so a simple random draw would contain too few to estimate agreement on them), and 60 coded studies. Human--human agreement on the same records is the baseline against which the human--LLM figures are read.

\subsection{Changes from earlier versions}
\label{sec:method-changes}
A pilot version of this review screened only Source A, using a language-model regular expression with title review, with one LLM screener and one LLM coder plus a second blinded pass. Protocol v2 added Source B, the abstract-level query, the NLP venues, snowballing and the recall audit; made all screening double with adjudication; added rules R4--R6; and introduced the ordered decision tree, the check-strength attribute, double coding, open-vocabulary entities and the web-verified artifact check. Of the \cnt{vone} studies included in the pilot, \cnt{vonekept} remain included; \cnt{vonedropped} are excluded under the stricter rules, mostly because their ``symbolic'' component is a neural module or retrieved text.

During revision we (i)~applied I1 consistently to AAAI special tracks and completed the NLP volumes from the ACL Anthology; (ii)~added the stage-2 query; (iii)~added a second screener to the recall audit and extended the audit; (iv)~added the known-item check from an independent survey; (v)~clarified R2 for optimisation solvers that only configure a system around the model (they do not interact with what the model reasons about); (vi)~added the supplementary coding, the full-text check, the second human rater and the sensitivity analyses. Stage-1 decisions were not changed.

\subsection{Tooling and reproducibility}
The pipeline uses only the Python standard library and \texttt{pdftotext}. \texttt{code/search/fetch\_sources.sh} retrieves the sources at the commits above, and \texttt{code/run\_all.py} rebuilds every derived file. The scripts check that every record has exactly the decisions the procedure requires: a missing or superfluous adjudication stops the run. Re-running the pipeline from the released decision files reproduces the search hits of both stages, the screened sets, the snowball candidates, the audit samples and every number in this paper exactly. The analysis is therefore reproducible; the LLM decisions are documented and auditable, but not regenerable. Appendix~\ref{app:replication} lists the files.
